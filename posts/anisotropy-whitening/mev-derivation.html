Step-by-step derivation connecting sample covariance, variance maximization, Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), and the MEV ratio.
Published

November 1, 2026

This page is a companion to Anisotropy and Whitening. It derives from scratch why the maximum explainable variance (MEV) along a single axis equals the ratio of squared singular values \(\sigma_1^2 / \sum_i \sigma_i^2\), and shows how singular values connect to the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\).

(Note on notation: in the main article and whitening derivation, \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) denotes the covariance matrix. Here we follow the standard SVD convention where \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is the rectangular diagonal matrix of singular values \(\sigma_i\), and we write \(\mathbf{S}\) for the sample covariance matrix.)

Step 1: the centred data matrix

Let \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) be a data matrix of \(n\) observations (rows). For instance, \(n\) contextualized occurrences of a word \(w\) across \(d\) embedding dimensions (columns).

Before measuring variance, we mean-centre each column. Let \(\bar{x}_j = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_{ij}\) be the mean of column \(j\), and replace every entry \(X_{ij}\) with \(X_{ij} - \bar{x}_j\). After centring, the mean of every column of \(\mathbf{X}\) is zero.

Step 2: deriving the exact sample covariance matrix

The sample covariance between two coordinates (columns) \(j\) and \(k\) is defined as:

\[ S_{jk} = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (X_{ij} - \bar{x}_j)(X_{ik} - \bar{x}_k). \]

Because we centred \(\mathbf{X}\) in Step 1, \(\bar{x}_j = 0\) and \(\bar{x}_k = 0\), so this simplifies to:

\[ S_{jk} = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n X_{ij} X_{ik}. \]

Notice that \(\sum_{i=1}^n X_{ij} X_{ik}\) is the dot product of column \(j\) and column \(k\) of \(\mathbf{X}\). Multiplying \(\mathbf{X}^\top\) (\(d \times n\)) by \(\mathbf{X}\) (\(n \times d\)) computes all column dot products at once. Therefore, the exact \(d \times d\) sample covariance matrix \(\mathbf{S}\) is:

\[ \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}. \]

Step 3: projecting the data onto a 1D axis

We want to compress our \(d\)-dimensional vectors onto a single 1D direction that retains as much spread as possible. Represent that direction by a unit column vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times 1}\):

\[ \mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v} = 1. \]

Projecting all \(n\) row vectors in \(\mathbf{X}\) onto \(\mathbf{v}\) is the matrix-vector product

\[ \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times 1}, \]

where \(z_i\) is the coordinate of the \(i\)-th observation along the axis \(\mathbf{v}\).

Step 4: variance of the projected data

Because the columns of \(\mathbf{X}\) have mean zero, their linear combination \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\) also has mean zero (\(\bar{z} = 0\)). The sample variance of the 1D projections \(z_1, \dots, z_n\) is therefore:

\[ \text{Var}(\mathbf{z}) = \frac{1}{n-1} \mathbf{z}^\top \mathbf{z}. \]

Substitute \(\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}\):

\[ \text{Var}(\mathbf{z}) = \frac{1}{n-1} (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v})^\top (\mathbf{X}\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{v}^\top \left( \frac{1}{n-1} \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} \right) \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}. \]

Step 5: maximizing variance via Lagrange multipliers

We want to choose \(\mathbf{v}\) to maximize \(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}\) subject to the unit-length constraint \(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v} = 1\). Form the Lagrangian with multiplier \(\lambda\):

\[ \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{v}, \lambda) = \mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v} - \lambda(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v} - 1). \]

Differentiate with respect to \(\mathbf{v}\) and set the gradient to \(\mathbf{0}\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{v}} = 2\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} - 2\lambda \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \implies \mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}. \]

This is the classic eigenvalue equation:

  • Any stationary direction \(\mathbf{v}\) must be an eigenvector of the covariance matrix \(\mathbf{S}\).
  • Left-multiplying both sides by \(\mathbf{v}^\top\) gives \(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{v} = \lambda\).

Because \(\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}\) is the variance of the projected data from Step 4, the eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is the exact variance explained by the direction \(\mathbf{v}\). To capture the maximum possible variance along a single axis, we pick the eigenvector \(\mathbf{v}_1\) corresponding to the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\). That vector is the first principal component, and the subsequent eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_d\) (with \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d \ge 0\)) capture the maximum remaining variance along orthogonal directions.

Step 6: connecting SVD to the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\)

Any real matrix \(\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) has a Singular Value Decomposition (SVD):

\[ \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top, \]

where:

  • \(\mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{U}^\top \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{I}_n\)), containing the left singular vectors as columns.
  • \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) is rectangular diagonal with non-negative singular values \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\) along its main diagonal.
  • \(\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is orthogonal (\(\mathbf{V}^\top \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^\top = \mathbf{I}_d\)), containing the right singular vectors as columns.

Substitute \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top\) into \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\):

\[ \mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top)^\top (\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top) = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^\top \underbrace{\mathbf{U}^\top \mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{I}_n} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^\top = \mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\mathbf{V}^\top. \]

Here \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\) is a \(d \times d\) diagonal matrix whose \(i\)-th diagonal entry is \(\sigma_i^2\). Right-multiplying both sides by \(\mathbf{V}\) (and using \(\mathbf{V}^\top \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{I}_d\)) gives:

\[ (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})\mathbf{V} = \mathbf{V}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}), \qquad\text{or column by column:}\qquad (\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X})\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i^2 \mathbf{v}_i. \]

Dividing by \(n - 1\) links this directly to our covariance matrix \(\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n-1}\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\):

\[ \mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}_i = \left(\frac{\sigma_i^2}{n-1}\right)\mathbf{v}_i. \]

Comparing this with \(\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i\) from Step 5 gives two fundamental identities:

  1. Principal components are right singular vectors: the columns \(\mathbf{v}_i\) of \(\mathbf{V}\) are the exact eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\) and of \(\mathbf{S}\).
  2. Singular values vs. eigenvalues: the squared singular values \(\sigma_i^2\) of \(\mathbf{X}\) are the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{X}^\top \mathbf{X}\), and the variance explained by the \(i\)-th principal component is \(\lambda_i = \frac{\sigma_i^2}{n-1}\).

Step 7: the Maximum Explainable Variance (MEV) formula

The proportion of total variance explained by the first \(k\) principal components is the sum of the first \(k\) variances divided by the total variance across all directions:

\[ \text{Explained Variance Ratio}(k) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i}{\sum_{i=1}^d \lambda_i} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k \frac{\sigma_i^2}{n-1}}{\sum_{i=1}^d \frac{\sigma_i^2}{n-1}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i^2}{\sum_{i=1}^d \sigma_i^2}. \]

Setting \(k = 1\) gives the exact proportion of variance captured by the single best 1D axis, which is Ethayarajh’s (2019) maximum explainable variance:

\[ \text{MEV} = \frac{\sigma_1^2}{\sum_{i} \sigma_i^2}. \]
